## Reference

Kassab, Amin, and Thomas Gries. 2026. “Energy transition and economic growth in low-income countries: the role of climate finance.” *Financial Innovation* 12(1), Article 159. Published 24 September 2026. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40854-026-00963-4.

Primary metadata: Paderborn University research portal, record 67256: https://ris.uni-paderborn.de/record/67256

## One-sentence reading

The paper gives a clean macroeconomic mechanism for a proposition close to our JETP argument: when an energy transition requires large up-front capital in a capital-scarce economy, **external climate finance can relax the aggregate capital constraint, whereas domestically financed transition investment can crowd out other productive capital and depress income during the transition**.

This is highly relevant to our paper, but it is not identical to our mechanism. Kassab & Gries model **capital scarcity / allocation**; our JETP argument is about **absorbability of externally offered finance given sovereign and utility solvency, debt-service constraints, instrument composition, and agency**.

## What the paper contributes

The paper studies a fossil-fuel-importing low-income economy in which renewable energy requires an up-front renewable capital stock. The transition can be financed either by international climate finance or by redirecting scarce domestic investment away from other productive uses.

Its central comparative result is:

1. sufficient and stable external climate finance permits renewable-capital accumulation without equivalent displacement of other productive capital;
2. domestic financing of the transition can crowd out non-energy productive investment and generate sizeable short- to medium-run income losses;
3. with an optimal long-run allocation of capital, renewable investment ultimately raises growth and welfare;
4. insufficient climate finance can leave fossil-energy demand growing with the economy, producing a rebound in fossil use and emissions.

The published abstract reports short- to medium-run income losses of **15–20%** in the domestic-finance/crowding-out case. That number is a model simulation, not an empirical estimate and should not travel into our paper except, if useful, as an illustration explicitly labelled as model-dependent.

## Why it matters for the JETP macro argument

Our historical design note says:

> “La macro est la thèse ; l’asymétrie entre bailleurs est son explication, jamais l’inverse.”

Kassab & Gries help make the first half of that statement economically precise. A transition package cannot be evaluated only by comparing its face value with an engineering investment need. The relevant macro question is what financing constraint the package relaxes, and at what cost.

A useful decomposition for our paper is therefore:

[
	ext{transition investment need}
ightarrow
	ext{financing instrument}
ightarrow
	ext{recipient balance-sheet effect}
ightarrow
	ext{domestic capital displacement / fiscal-space use}
ightarrow
	ext{feasible absorption}.
]

Kassab & Gries are strongest on the middle arrow: climate finance changes the opportunity cost of renewable investment relative to other domestic capital formation.

Our paper adds what their stylised model appears not to capture directly: the external finance itself may be **non-absorbable** when it is predominantly debt. A loan does not relax the recipient constraint one-for-one if it worsens debt service, violates debt rules, creates contingent liabilities, exposes a utility to foreign-exchange risk, or requires a solvent borrower/counterparty that does not want the loan.

That gives us a sharper formulation than “more climate finance is needed”:

> **The macroeconomic value of climate finance is not its gross face value but the extent to which its terms relax the recipient economy’s binding capital, fiscal, external or balance-sheet constraint without creating a tighter constraint elsewhere.**

This sentence is a candidate framing proposition, not yet manuscript text.

## Capital crowding-out versus JETP absorbability

The distinction should be explicit because the two mechanisms can otherwise be conflated.

| Mechanism | Kassab & Gries | Our JETP paper |
|---|---|---|
| Scarce domestic saving/capital | Central | Relevant background constraint |
| Renewable capital competes with other productive capital | Central | One possible opportunity cost |
| External finance relaxes domestic capital constraint | Central | Conditional, not automatic |
| Sovereign debt-service capacity | Not established from accessible material | Central |
| Utility / SOE solvency and counterparty risk | Not established | Central in several cases |
| Grant vs loan composition | External finance is analytically beneficial; instrument heterogeneity needs full-text check | Central |
| FX exposure / denomination | Not established | Relevant, country-specific |
| Borrower agency / refusal | Not central | Central |
| Aggregate package has no single evaluator | Outside the model | Central institutional explanation |
| Non-absorption despite announced finance | Not the main object | Core empirical outcome |

The key point is therefore **not** to cite Kassab & Gries as direct evidence that JETP loans were non-absorbable. They provide a theoretical reason why the financing source matters for development during transition. Our comparative evidence asks whether the actual JETP instruments relaxed the relevant constraint in the four recipient countries.

## A useful extension of our “solvency buys agency” proposition

The existing hypothesis is bimodal:

- sufficiently solvent actors can refuse unattractive debt;
- weakly solvent actors may need finance but be unable to service or absorb it.

Kassab & Gries add a third macro margin between these extremes: even where debt can technically be serviced, transition investment financed from scarce domestic resources may impose a large **opportunity cost through displaced capital formation**.

This suggests distinguishing three questions in the absorbability test:

1. **Can the recipient borrow?** — solvency, debt rules, fiscal space, utility balance sheet.
2. **Should the recipient borrow on these terms?** — concessionality, currency, maturity, contingent liabilities, expected return.
3. **What does the financing displace?** — domestic public investment, private capital formation, development spending, or alternative energy investment.

This is potentially stronger than using debt/GDP alone as the macro screen.

## Mapping to the four JETP cases

### Vietnam

The paper provides theoretical support for treating Vietnam’s debt constraint as economically substantive rather than as mere reluctance to deploy climate finance. If the alternative to external borrowing is not “no investment” but domestic financing that competes with industrial and infrastructure capital, then the policy problem is the terms on which external finance relaxes that constraint. Our case still differs sharply from the stylised low-income-country setup: Vietnam has substantial domestic saving, a large banking system, state-owned utilities and explicit public-debt discipline.

### Indonesia

The crowding-out logic is relevant because the energy transition competes with a large domestic industrialisation and infrastructure agenda. But Indonesia’s issue is not simply scarcity of finance. National industrial policy can redirect or reshape the transition itself; agency and sectoral priorities remain essential.

### South Africa

This is where the distinction between **aggregate capital scarcity** and **balance-sheet-specific constraints** is clearest. External finance can be available while Eskom’s financial condition, sovereign exposure and the allocation of liabilities determine whether it can actually be used for the intended transition.

### Senegal

Kassab & Gries are closest to the macro intuition of a capital-scarce, import-dependent economy. But Senegal also illustrates why their favourable treatment of external climate finance cannot be transferred mechanically: a predominantly loan-financed package can relax the current investment constraint while tightening future fiscal and debt-service constraints.

## Relation to prior literature: novelty should be stated carefully

Kassab & Gries should not be presented as the first paper to show that external financing can reduce transition-related crowding-out.

A close prior comparator is the IMF Departmental Paper **“Harnessing Renewables in Sub-Saharan Africa – Barriers, Reforms, and Economic Prospects”** (2024). Using the IMF-ENV dynamic CGE model, it explicitly compares domestic and external financing of renewable expansion and reports that relying on domestic financing dampens macroeconomic gains because renewable investment crowds out other sectors.

So the useful novelty for us is narrower:

- a transparent long-run growth-theory treatment of the mechanism;
- an explicit link between capital scarcity, renewable-capital accumulation and climate finance;
- a bridge from the energy-finance literature to our empirical question of whether a JETP package actually relaxes the relevant recipient constraint.

For literature positioning, cite Kassab & Gries alongside, not instead of, the IMF 2024 macro work.

## What we can use in the paper

### Strong use

Use Kassab & Gries to motivate the proposition that **the financing mode of the transition is macroeconomically consequential even when the underlying renewable investment is productive in the long run**.

This supports a short theoretical bridge in Q3 (“why?”) before our empirical move from capital scarcity to debt/instrument absorbability.

### Moderate use

Use the paper to justify adding an opportunity-cost dimension to the ex-ante absorbability table: not only package/PIB, debt and utility solvency, but also whether financing is external and genuinely additional or requires domestic co-financing / balance-sheet capacity.

### Weak or unsafe use

Do not use:

- the 15–20% simulated income loss as a transferable magnitude;
- the paper as evidence that JETPs failed because of crowding-out;
- “external finance” as synonymous with “grant” or “constraint-relaxing finance”;
- a claim that any dollar of foreign lending is additional to domestic capital formation.

## Implication for our conceptual language

The paper suggests tightening the macro vocabulary around **constraint-relaxing finance**.

Gross announced climate finance (F) is not the economically relevant object. A more useful conceptual quantity is the part that relaxes the binding recipient constraint after allowing for debt service, co-financing and displaced investment:

[
F^{*}
=
F
-
	ext{future debt-service burden}
-
	ext{required domestic co-financing}
-
	ext{domestic capital displaced}
-
	ext{other balance-sheet costs}.
]

This is only a heuristic decomposition, not an accounting identity. It is useful because it makes clear why two packages with the same face value can have very different macroeconomic effects.

For the JETP paper, **“constraint-relaxing finance”** may be preferable to “effective climate finance”: the latter risks confusion with grant-equivalent accounting and climate-finance effectiveness literatures.

## Main caveats

1. **Model, not identification.** The 15–20% result is generated by a calibrated theoretical model; it is not a causal estimate from observed transitions.
2. **External-finance closure is decisive.** We need the full text before relying on how external climate finance enters the resource constraint, current account and intertemporal budget constraint.
3. **Debt versus transfer.** The abstract does not establish whether climate finance is modelled as debt with repayment, concessional lending, grants or an exogenous capital inflow. This is the single most important issue for our use.
4. **No obvious sovereign/utility balance sheet.** Our JETP mechanism depends precisely on the heterogeneous borrowers and liabilities hidden by an aggregate “external finance” variable.
5. **Low-income-country scope.** Three of the four JETP cases are structurally different from the representative import-dependent low-income economy that motivates the model.
6. **Existing macro result.** IMF work already obtains a domestic-financing crowding-out result in a richer CGE framework, so Kassab & Gries should be used for mechanism and clarity rather than priority claims.

## Full-text audit to do before manuscript insertion

Once the article PDF/HTML is available, check specifically:

1. the household/firm saving and investment closure;
2. whether the economy is genuinely open to private capital or only to exogenous climate-finance transfers;
3. whether climate finance is a grant, loan, stock or flow and whether repayment appears;
4. the balance-of-payments closure and treatment of imported fossil fuel;
5. whether renewable capital goods are domestic or imported;
6. the production-function substitutability between conventional capital, labour and energy;
7. how the optimal capital allocation is defined;
8. calibration countries/data behind the reported 15–20%;
9. sensitivity of the crowding-out result to savings, depreciation, energy prices and finance persistence;
10. whether debt sustainability, sovereign risk, exchange rates or utility balance sheets enter anywhere.

The answers determine whether Kassab & Gries can be used only as motivation or as a formal theoretical benchmark for the JETP macro section.

## Bottom line for paper 2

**Keep.** Kassab & Gries give us a useful theoretical bridge from “energy transition requires finance” to “the macroeconomic effect depends on what constraint the finance actually relaxes.” But our contribution should move one step further:

> **JETPs offered external finance, but mostly as loans. The empirical question is therefore not whether external finance can prevent domestic crowding-out in principle; it is whether the instruments actually offered were balance-sheet-relaxing and absorbable for the intended sovereigns and counterparties.**

That formulation places Kassab & Gries upstream of our comparative mechanism rather than in competition with it.
