# Causal design options for "Did JETPs accelerate energy-project progress?"

Report for the 0729 design dossier. Nothing here is a chosen design. Ranking is provisional and the conditions that would reverse it are stated.

## Verdict first

A credible causal paper is feasible only under a narrowed claim. The defensible target is the **total partnership effect on the speed of financing transitions in the eligible energy pipeline of a JETP country**, estimated by a longitudinal cross-country comparison with contemporaneous untreated beneficiary countries, with the sector-gap (triple-difference) contrast as the main robustness check rather than the headline. The **project-support effect** (does JETP labelling speed up a given project) is the more intuitive question but the harder one: labelling is plausibly selected on maturity, and the within-country control group is itself exposed to the partnership. It should be pursued only as a stage-matched target-trial emulation and reported as a relative, conditional effect.

Two facts drive this verdict. First, there are four treated countries, three of them treated within thirteen months of each other, and one (Senegal) with a different partner set. Country-level inference must therefore be permutation-based and the paper must be honest that it can only detect large effects. Second, the outcome most likely to be observable on both sides with a common clock is the approval → signature → effectiveness sequence at multilateral lenders, not identification → approval. If the feasibility exercise below shows pipeline entry is unrecoverable for historical and comparator cases, the paper narrows to post-approval transitions and states plainly that it cannot speak to preparation speed.

Verified anchor dates for treatment onset: South Africa declaration 2 November 2021; Indonesia 15 November 2022; Viet Nam 14 December 2022; Senegal 22 June 2023 ([EIB press release](https://www.eib.org/en/press/all/2023-242-senegal-and-international-partners-announce-a-just-energy-transition-partnership-combining-climate-and-development-objectives)). Anticipation began earlier: the G7 under the German presidency agreed in June 2022 to pursue partnerships with Indonesia, Senegal, Viet Nam and India, and the India negotiation was later discontinued ([Clean Energy Wire](https://www.cleanenergywire.org/news/india-donor-countries-give-just-energy-transition-partnership-german-official)). The US withdrew from the South Africa, Indonesia and Viet Nam partnerships in March 2025 ([Climate Home News](https://www.climatechangenews.com/2025/03/07/us-withdraws-from-coal-to-clean-jetp-deals-for-developing-nations/)). These give three distinct event dates per country (anticipation, declaration, investment-plan publication) and one adverse shock, which matters for design.

## Portfolio of candidate designs

Two estimands must be separated before any of these is chosen, because they need different treatments, time zeros and sampling frames.

- **E1, partnership effect.** Treatment is the country's entry into a JETP, applied to every energy operation at risk in that country from the onset date. Unit of assignment is the country. It answers "did the partnership change how fast the country's energy pipeline moved" and cannot say which projects benefited.
- **E2, project-support effect.** Treatment is a project's admission to JETP support (appearance in the investment plan, or IPG-attributed financing). Unit of assignment is the project. It answers "did support change this project's speed relative to similar unsupported projects in the same country and period" and cannot recover the total effect if unsupported projects were also affected.

The naive comparison in the ticket's first test (JETP-labelled projects versus old completed projects) fails both estimands. It conditions on completion, which drops slow and cancelled cases, restarts the clock at labelling, which creates immortal time in the sense of [Suissa (2008)](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article-abstract/167/4/492/233064), and confounds the partnership with the post-2021 calendar. Every design below is built to avoid those three faults.

**Common outcome definition.** For each stage transition, define the outcome as the cumulative incidence of reaching the next stage within a fixed horizon (12, 24, 36 months) among all operations at risk at the start of the interval, with dropping or cancellation as a competing event and the observation cutoff as right-censoring. Interval-dated events enter as intervals, not midpoints. This retains pending and withdrawn cases and makes durations among completers a secondary statistic only. Aggregated to country-by-half-year cohorts, the same quantity becomes the panel outcome for country-level designs.

Notes on each design beyond the table.

- **A is the workhorse.** It needs, for each country and half-year since roughly 2012, the stage outcomes above. World Bank and ADB records carry approval, signing and effectiveness dates; ADB project data sheets and completion reports also carry concept-clearance dates ([ADB project cycle](https://www.adb.org/what-we-do/public-sector-financing/project-cycle)). Whether identification dates exist for historical World Bank cases depends on archived Monthly Operational Summaries, which listed pipeline projects from identification to signing and have been discontinued in favour of the Operations Portal ([World Bank MOS page](https://projects.worldbank.org/en/projects-operations/products-and-services/brief/monthly-operational-summary)). Archived MOS issues in Documents and Reports are the only route to dropped historical proposals. Inference must use the small-number-of-policy-changes approach of [Conley and Taber (2011)](https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article/93/1/113/57888/Inference-with-Difference-in-Differences-with-a) or permutation across donor countries, and pre-trend violations should be bounded with [Rambachan and Roth (2023)](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/90/5/2555/7039335) rather than tested away.
- **B is a presentation of A, not a separate identification strategy.** [Abadie (2021)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjel.20191450) requires a long, stable pre-period outcome series and a donor pool free of contamination. Half-yearly cohort outcomes from 2012 give roughly 18 to 22 pre-periods for South Africa and fewer for Senegal. Stage-attainment shares in small pipelines are noisy, so the fit will be imperfect and the demeaned variant is the relevant one. Treat B as a sensitivity display with placebo-in-space p-values, not as the headline.
- **C is attractive but its identifying restriction is the one most likely to fail.** South Africa's electricity crisis and Eskom reforms in 2022 to 2023, Indonesia's PLN and coal early-retirement debates, and Viet Nam's PDP8 in 2023 are energy-specific changes coincident with JETP. Non-energy operations may also be exposed through lender staff capacity, fiscal-space conditionality and public-finance reforms bundled into the partnership. If exposure is material, C identifies energy-minus-non-energy, and the paper must say so. Cross-sector comparability requires replacing technology matching with baseline scale, complexity and procurement type, and flagging mixed operations before outcomes are inspected.
- **D is the only design that addresses selection on ambition directly.** It requires reconstructing the selection funnel from contemporaneous sources: G7 communiqués, IPG statements, reporting on candidate countries, and parallel programmes. Two leads I recall but did not verify in this session, and which the audit should check: the Climate Investment Funds' Accelerating Coal Transition programme reportedly selected India, Indonesia, the Philippines and South Africa in 2021, and ADB's Energy Transition Mechanism piloted in Indonesia, the Philippines and Viet Nam. If confirmed, the Philippines and India become "selected for coal transition but no JETP" comparators, which is far more credible than income-matched countries. Treat this as a hypothesis until sourced.
- **E is the only route to E2.** The critical protocol elements are a fixed admission date per country, a risk set defined on that date, and a pre-entry maturity vector (dates of stages already completed before admission) used both for matching and for a falsification check: if admitted projects had systematically faster pre-entry transitions than unadmitted ones, selection on speed is demonstrated and the unconfoundedness assumption is not credible. The [Hernán and Robins (2016)](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article-abstract/183/8/758/1739860) target-trial framework provides the template for aligning eligibility, assignment and follow-up start.

## Threats, discovery, feasibility and rejection rules

**Cross-cutting threats and how each design handles them.**

- Timing and anticipation. Use the earliest documented negotiation date as onset for A to C and drop the anticipation window in a sensitivity run. Multiple dated events per country (G7 commitment, declaration, investment plan) permit an event-study shape check: an effect that appears before the first documented contact is a pre-trend, not an effect.
- Pre-existing maturity. For E1 it is a composition variable, not a confounder, and must not be adjusted away. For E2 it is the main confounder and enters the risk-set definition.
- Instrument change as mediator. Grants and guarantees may clear faster than sovereign loans. Do not condition on post-onset instrument mix when estimating E1. A separate decomposition can report how much of any total effect runs through instrument mix, labelled as mediation with its own assumptions.
- Unfinished and cancelled cases. Cumulative incidence with competing risks handles them. Approved-only databases cannot recover the probability of approval, so identification → approval is estimable only where pipeline archives exist on both sides.
- Comparable definitions. Board approval, signature and effectiveness are legally defined at MDBs and comparable across countries. Bilateral committee approvals are not, so bilaterals enter through IATI activity dates as a secondary frame with their own flag.
- Few treated countries. Inference at the country level, always. Project counts do not add independent assignments. Report a minimum detectable effect from pre-period country variance before looking at post-period outcomes.
- Spillovers. Within-country designs (E, F, and the sector contrast in C) identify relative effects. Cross-country designs are vulnerable if IPG lenders shifted staff or budget away from comparator countries; check comparator-country energy approvals for a post-2022 dip.
- Resources shifted to or from non-energy sectors. This is what makes non-energy operations exposed. Audit each JETP country's non-energy lending volumes and conditionality after onset before deciding whether C is admissible.

**How to discover assignment evidence.** Do not assume a cutoff, instrument or exogenous rollout. Search for: dated G7 and IPG statements naming candidate countries; reporting on countries that declined or stalled; selection documents of parallel coal-transition programmes; lender country-partnership frameworks that reference JETP before declaration; and any IPG internal criteria disclosed in parliamentary or audit-office reports. The output is a dated funnel table with source locators. If the funnel yields at least two credible non-concluded countries, D becomes the preferred comparator frame for A.

**Smallest useful feasibility exercise.** One exercise, roughly two weeks of extraction, decides whether A is viable and whether E2 is meaningful.

1. Build the country × half-year panel of approval → signature and approval → effectiveness cumulative incidence at 12 and 24 months for World Bank and ADB energy operations approved 2012 to 2025, in the four JETP countries and a written candidate pool of 25 to 40 borrowers matched on 2019 to 2020 income group, lending eligibility and electricity-sector indicators. Use only dates available from project pages and data sheets. Record whether effectiveness dates are actually exposed by the World Bank API or only inside documents.
2. Plot pre-onset series and compute the pre-period between-country standard deviation. Derive the minimum detectable effect under permutation inference. If a plausible JETP effect is below that threshold, the causal paper cannot deliver a decisive estimate and the author should know before further extraction.
3. Run the planned three-case documentary check and, for the 10 to 15 JETP cases, record pre-entry maturity and the admission date. Compare pre-entry stage durations of admitted versus unadmitted same-country projects. This is the selection-on-maturity falsification for E.
4. Probe archived Monthly Operational Summaries for two countries and two years to establish whether dropped pipeline entries are recoverable. If not, identification → approval is out of scope for the causal paper.

**Criteria for rejecting or narrowing the causal claim.** State these before outcomes are inspected.

- Reject A and B for a stage if fewer than eight stable pre-onset periods exist for that stage in the treated countries, or if the comparator pool contains no country within the treated countries' pre-period range on the outcome.
- Narrow A to a bounded claim if pre-trend differences exceed the effect size the paper would call meaningful, using the Rambachan and Roth breakdown value rather than a pre-trend test.
- Reject C if non-energy lending in JETP countries shows a discrete change at onset that is not seen in comparator countries, or if the sector-gap pre-trend diverges.
- Reject E as a causal design if admitted projects had faster pre-entry transitions than unadmitted ones and no stage-matched risk set can balance this. Report the descriptive gap only as an observatory output.
- Reject the paper's causal path for a stage if the minimum detectable effect exceeds any effect the literature or the partnership documents would consider plausible.
- Null and adverse effects remain reportable under every design.

**Provisional ranking and what would reverse it.**

1. A with D's funnel-based comparator frame, outcome approval → signature and approval → effectiveness, permutation inference, Rambachan-Roth bounds. Reversed if the funnel cannot be documented (falls back to income-matched pool, weaker) or if comparator-country energy pipelines show contamination.
2. C as primary robustness, elevated to headline only if the feasibility audit finds no energy-specific coincident shock in the treated countries and non-energy lending unaffected. Reversed downward if South African or Indonesian non-energy lending moved at onset.
3. E for the project-support question, reported as a conditional relative effect. Reversed upward if pre-entry maturity is balanced between admitted and unadmitted projects, which would make E2 clean and arguably more interesting than E1.
4. B as a display of A. Reversed upward if the pre-period series turn out long and smooth.
5. F and G as sensitivity and future work respectively.

Recommendation on scope: plan for a NARROW outcome. The causal paper most likely to survive review estimates the partnership effect on post-approval financing transitions with contemporaneous comparators and bounded parallel trends, reports the project-support contrast as conditional and relative, and explicitly declines to claim an effect on preparation speed unless pipeline archives are recovered. That is a smaller claim than the original question, but it is one the data can bear.

## Fable reconsideration after cross-review

The coordinator supplied Astra's specific objections after the independent reports were complete. Fable 5.1 returned the following response; the initial report above remains unchanged.

Reconsidering the five issues without new evidence. Revisions are marked where I concede.

**1. Permutation inference. Revised.** "Must be permutation-based" was overstated, and the p-values I implied are not randomization p-values. With politically selected countries, placebo-in-space permutation only answers "how atypical is the treated country's post-onset deviation relative to comparator deviations under the same model". That is valid only if residual shocks are exchangeable across countries conditional on the model, not if assignment is exchangeable. Political selection breaks it precisely when selection tracks a shock, such as an energy crisis. What justifies reported numbers is therefore model-based: Conley and Taber's approach, which assumes the treated country's idiosyncratic shock is drawn from the same distribution as comparator residuals, plus Rambachan and Roth bounds for trend violations. The paper should report placebo ranks and bounded intervals, label them conditional on parallel trends and residual exchangeability, and never call them exact. Permuting only within a documented funnel set makes exchangeability more plausible but leaves a reference distribution of two or three points, so it is a robustness display, not the main inference.

**2. Cohort and estimand. Revised, Astra is right.** Cohorts defined by approval date condition on a post-treatment event. If JETP changes which or how many projects reach approval, post-onset approved cohorts are treatment-selected and the approval-to-signature effect is confounded with composition. The corrected structure is a rolling landmark panel: at each half-year date t, the risk set is every operation pending at that stage in that country on that date, and the outcome is attainment within the horizon. The country-by-half-year panel I proposed survives, but rows must be indexed by risk-set date, not approval date. This also answers the mechanical-zero point: pre-onset attainment is not zero because pre-onset rows come from earlier landmarks, each with its own follow-up. Approval counts per period become a separate outcome with no conditioning. Comparators must be contemporaneous, not historical, because calendar shocks were my original objection to the naive test. Where only approved operations are observable, the estimand shrinks to "among approved operations, time to effectiveness", which is descriptive and must be labelled so.

**3. Maturity. Revised.** I conflated two things. Baseline maturity is the stage distribution of the risk set fixed on the landmark date, before onset. It is a pre-treatment covariate, and balancing or adjusting for it improves comparability without removing effect. Treatment-induced composition is the set of operations that enter or are reshaped after onset. Adjusting for that removes part of the effect and is what my "must not adjust away" sentence should have targeted. For E2 the same line applies: pre-admission stage durations are confounders, post-admission progress is outcome.

**4. Funnel. Partly conceded.** The funnel does not identify assignment. Countries that entered and exited differ in the reasons for exit, and India's exit over coal is exactly the kind of political and energy constraint that plausibly correlates with pipeline speed. India was also exposed through eighteen months of negotiation, so it is partially treated. What the funnel does is narrow selection to fewer dimensions by removing selection on lender attention and stated ambition at entry. The residual selection must be documented and argued, not assumed away. The better funnel is programme selection independent of JETP negotiation, such as the Philippines under coal-transition programmes, if the audit confirms it. Design D is a comparability frame with stated residual selection, not an identification strategy, and the ranking should say so.

**5. Synthetic control and the thresholds. Revised on both.** Calling B a display of A was wrong. Synthetic control replaces parallel trends with a factor-model assumption and weighted donors, so it tests a different form of the counterfactual. It earns a place as an independent check, weakened by imperfect fit. The eight-period and 25 to 40 country figures were exploratory targets, not derived criteria. The real criteria are pre-period fit relative to the effect size the paper would call meaningful, common support on the outcome, and a minimum detectable effect computed before post-period inspection. My rejection rules also conflated identification with power. Contamination or missing common support rejects a design. A large minimum detectable effect does not; the estimate is still identified and should be reported with its interval and described as inconclusive if it is.

**Astra's three notes.** On Senegal, my sentence was wrong: from 2012, Senegal's June 2023 onset gives more half-years than South Africa's November 2021, not fewer. What I meant was that Senegal's thinner pipeline gives noisier per-period shares, which is a precision problem, not a period-count problem. On balance, agreed: balanced pre-entry maturity between admitted and unadmitted projects is a necessary falsification check, and passing it cannot establish unconfoundedness. My "reversed upward if balanced" was too strong and should read "not rejected if balanced". On immortal time, agreed: resetting the clock at labelling is not itself immortal time. The bias arises only when the comparison origin is approval while treatment is defined later, so labelled projects must survive the pre-labelling interval that comparators are not required to survive. If both clocks start at analogous dates, the fault in the naive test is selection on completion and maturity, and the report should name it that way.

Net effect on the ranking: A survives only in rolling-landmark form with model-based inference. D drops to a comparator frame. B rises to an independent check. Rejection rules split into identification failures and power reports.
